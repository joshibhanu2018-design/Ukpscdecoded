## Part A: Major Economic Problems

## 25.1 Poverty: Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)

### State-Level Progress

Uttarakhand has made significant progress in poverty reduction. The MPI headcount ratio declined from 9.7 per cent in 2021-22 to 6.92 per cent in 2024-25, down from over 40 per cent at the time of the state's formation. On the SDG India Index, Uttarakhand holds Rank 1, tied with Kerala, and is among the best-performing states nationally on the MPI.

### The Hill-Plain Poverty Divide

| Category | Districts | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| High Deprivation | Haridwar (urban slum poverty), Almora (rural mountain blocks) | Nutrition gaps, fuel deprivation |
| Low Deprivation | Dehradun (HDI 0.765), Nainital | Service jobs, tourism, state capital spending |

### Core Deprivation Metrics (MPI Dimensions)

| Dimension | Data | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| Nutrition | 32% rural hill households experience deficits | Erratic fresh food supply in mountains |
| Clean Fuel | 28% high-altitude HHs rely on biomass | LPG cylinder transport cost prohibitive |
| Sanitation/Housing | 14% deprivation in basic housing | Forest laws restrict building material access |
| Education | Improving but hill schools face single-digit enrolment | Migration-driven demand collapse |

Uttarakhand's poverty is not caloric starvation but structural multi-dimensional deprivation encompassing nutrition logistics failure, energy access gaps, and terrain-induced isolation. Standard income metrics understate the real vulnerability of hill communities.

## 25.2 Migration: The Economic Drain

### Scale of the Crisis

Over 1,100 villages have been completely abandoned (ghost villages with zero population), and more than 3,000 villages have experienced over 50 per cent population decline. Between 2018 and 2022, an average of 193 people migrated out of the state daily. One in three households (27.8 per cent, rising to 34.3 per cent in hill areas) has at least one migrant member. The primary driver is employment search (38.93 per cent of cases), and 75.5 per cent of migrants send remittances home.

### The "Hollowing-Out" Economic Cycle

The cycle begins with the out-migration of working-age youth, which leads to the abandonment of agricultural terraces and consequent soil erosion. This causes a drop in the local tax base and productivity, creating a money-order economy with high local inflation, which in turn drives further out-migration, reinforcing the cycle.

### Economic Consequences

The remittance trap means cash goes to consumption rather than local capital creation, producing inflation without growth. Farm abandonment leads to topsoil erosion, Lantana overgrowth, and increased human-wildlife conflict. Plain-bound overcrowding causes land prices to surge in Haldwani and Dehradun while public services reach capacity. The feminization of agriculture means women constitute 60 per cent of agricultural labour and perform 72 per cent of water collection. Border districts are depopulating, creating a strategic vacuum and national security risk. The hills are also losing demographic weight, creating a future delimitation threat and political marginalization.

### Sectoral Impact

Infrastructure sees recurring repair bills with maintenance costs three to four times higher than in the plains. Tourism suffers revenue disruption from helicopter crashes and landslides during the Char Dham season. Agriculture faces crop destruction, livestock death, and livelihood loss. The energy sector contends with hydro turbines damaged by siltation and transmission lines being cut.

### The Reconstruction Trap

Every major disaster forces the state to redirect development capital expenditure into emergency reconstruction. This creates a "treadmill effect" — building, destroying, rebuilding — that prevents long-term asset accumulation in hill districts.

## 25.4 Environmental Degradation: Economic Loss

### The "Hidden" Cost

The estimated forest fire loss per hectare is Rs 1,30,387 on average, with actual losses 65 to 90 times higher than official estimates. The loss breakdown comprises timber (59 per cent), tree regeneration (15 per cent), fuelwood and fodder (10 per cent), and wild edibles (7 per cent). The Kedarnath Wildlife Sanctuary's ecosystem value dropped from approximately USD 134 million in 2001 to approximately USD 113.68 million in 2024. The Terai Arc Landscape provides ecosystem services worth Rs 390 billion (USD 6 billion) annually, which are threatened by industrial expansion. Approximately 45,000 hectares of forest land have been diverted for non-forest uses since 2000. Annual Pirul (pine needle) production reaches 23 lakh metric tonnes, which is a major fire catalyst.

### Key Environmental Threats

| Threat | Economic Implication |
|---|---|
| Glacial Retreat | Long-term hydropower potential threatened; Pindari retreating 8-10m/year |
| Spring Crisis | 50%+ springs drying; women's burden increases; agri-productivity drops |

| Forest Fires | 10,000+ sq km highly vulnerable; destroys regeneration capacity |
| Carrying Capacity Breach | Tourism pressure (7 Cr tourists/1 Cr population); lake eutrophication |

## 25.5 Human-Wildlife Conflict: The Migration-HWC Feedback Loop

### The Destructive Cycle

The cycle begins with declining agrarian capital due to migration and reduced crop protection, leading to ecosystem degradation through fallow land and invasive weed overgrowth. This causes an extension of wildlife foraging zones as animals approach villages, resulting in severe crop raids and loss of human life. The outcome is total abandonment of agriculture and further migration, which reinforces the cycle.

### Key Mechanisms

Fallow land acts as wildlife corridors, as abandoned fields become Lantana cover providing wild boar and leopard habitat. The loss of community guarding means the elderly cannot maintain night vigils and crops become easy targets. The shift to cash crops such as potatoes and peas attracts wildlife more than traditional millets. Drying springs force animals to leave forests seeking water, bringing them into villages.

### Economic Consequences

Compensation for death in human-wildlife conflict has been raised from Rs 6 lakh to Rs 10 lakh. A single wild boar raid can destroy an entire season's potato crop. Ineffective barrier fencing drains household savings. Repeated losses cause farming to be abandoned, triggering ghost village formation.

### Solutions (Strategic)

| Solution | Details |
|---|---|
| Bio-Shield Barriers | Lemongrass, ginger, garlic borders — wildlife-resistant + income-generating |
| Solar Fencing (Community) | Youth cooperatives maintain solar-powered smart fencing |
| Invasive Scrub Clearance | Use VB-G RAM G funds to clear Lantana and remove wildlife cover |
| Transit Centres (CAMPA) | Rs 14 Crore for 7 centres (Pauri, Tehri, Uttarkashi, Chamoli, Dehradun, US Nagar, Bageshwar) |
| Animal Rescue Centres | Rs 5 Crore under CAMPA |

## 25.9 Food Security and PDS

The state's Public Distribution System covers approximately 23.67 lakh ration cardholders across three categories: AAY (Pink, ultra poor) with 1.84 lakh families receiving 35 kg per card; PHH (White, NFSA) with 12.14 lakh families receiving 5 kg per unit; and SFY (Yellow, State) with 9.69 lakh families receiving 7.5 kg per card. The system operates through 9,063 Fair Price Shops, all equipped with e-PoS (100 per cent automated, biometric). One Nation One Ration Card was implemented in 2020. A Grain ATM has been piloted in Dehradun in collaboration with UN-WFP. The Devbhoomi Parivar ID serves as the digital PDS backbone, enabling ration portability.

## 25.10 Soldiers' Welfare (Sainik Kalyan)

### Him Prahari Scheme

The Him Prahari scheme aims to settle ex-servicemen in ghost villages along the China and Nepal border. It serves a dual objective: stopping migration and creating a first line of defence. The scheme targets ex-servicemen and local youth in Pithoragarh, Chamoli, and Uttarkashi, providing settlement assistance and a "Border Guard" honorarium (approximately Rs 5.45 crore pilot). Participants serve as "eyes and ears" for security agencies while also providing community monitoring.

### UPNL (Uttarakhand Purv Sainik Kalyan Nigam)

The UPNL budget for 2026-27 is Rs 289.98 crore. Its purpose is to ensure equal pay for equal work for contract workers, who are mostly ex-servicemen dependents. The corporation provides vocational training, skill rehabilitation, and civilian re-employment services to the approximately 1.38 lakh veterans in the state.

### Other Soldiers' Welfare Measures

Financial grants for gallantry award winners have been hiked. The compassionate appointment window has been extended from 2 to 5 years, with 28 martyrs' families given jobs. Free state transport has been codified for gallantry winners and ex-servicemen. The Eco-Task Force deploys military-led afforestation battalions.

## 25.11 Disaster Rehabilitation

### U-PREPARE Project

The U-PREPARE project is funded at Rs 1,480 crore by the World Bank for the period 2024 to 2029. It focuses on pre-disaster structural resilience rather than just post-disaster relief. Targets include retrofitting 45 bridges, improving 8 roads, constructing 10 disaster shelters, building 19 fire stations, and constructing an SDRF building.

### Community Resilience

The Yuva Aapda Mitra and Aapda Sakhi programmes have trained 20,000 local youth and women in search, rescue, and first aid. The Nabh Netra Drone is used for post-disaster damage assessment where physical access is cut off. The SACHET App implements the Common Alerting Protocol for lightning, avalanche, and flood warnings. Joshimath rehabilitation involves slope stabilization and relocation to safe-zone townships at Gauchar.
