# CHAPTER 3

# The Chand Dynasty of Kumaon & Gorkha Rule in Uttarakhand

## PART A: THE CHAND DYNASTY OF KUMAON (700 CE – 1790 CE)

## 3.1 FOUNDATION & RISE

The Chand Dynasty was founded by Somchand, who arrived from Jhansi (Allahabad) and gained power by marrying the daughter of the last Katyuri ruler Brahmadev. He established his first capital at Champawat, where he constructed the Rajbunga Fort. To administer his new kingdom, Somchand appointed four Kiledars, collectively known as the **'Char Al'** — Karki, Bora, Tadagi, and Chaudhary. In its early phase, the Chand kingdom was subordinate to the Doti (Nepal) kings.

A major transformation came in 1563 CE when King Balo Kalyan Chand shifted the capital to Almora (then called Alamnagar), where he built the Lalmundi Fort (later known as Fort Moira).

## 3.3 KING RUDRA CHAND (1565–1597 CE) — THE SCHOLAR KING

Rudra Chand was the most multifaceted ruler of the Chand dynasty, balancing military might with scholarly and social contributions.

### Mughal Relations

Rudra Chand was a contemporary of Mughal Emperor Akbar, whom he personally met in Lahore in 1588 to accept suzerainty. As a result, Kumaon was listed under the Delhi Suba in Abul Fazl's *Ain-i-Akbari*. He also founded the town of Rudrapur.

### Social Engineering — Traivarnik Dharmanirnay

Rudra Chand fundamentally restructured Kumaoni society by composing the *Traivarnik Dharmanirnay*. Under this system, Brahmins were divided into strict hierarchical classes: the Chauthani (highest tier), the Tithani or Panchbirriye (middle tier), the Pitaliye or Haliye (lower tier), and the uniquely created Oli Brahmin class, whose sole duty was to warn the public by beating plates during hailstorms.

### Literary Works

Rudra Chand authored three significant texts: *Yatha Usharagodaya* on political administration, *Shyenik Shastra* on the art of hunting, and *Yayati Charitram*, a Sanskrit drama.

### Military & Architecture

Rudra Chand fought the Badhanagarh (Gwaldam) War against Garhwal ruler Balbhadra Shah, in which his commander Purush Pant was killed. He also built the Malla Mahal fort in Almora.

## 3.4 CHAND ADMINISTRATION & JUSTICE SYSTEM

### Territorial Division

The Chand state was organized hierarchically. The King served as supreme authority over the entire state. Below him, the territory was divided into Mandals (ruled directly or by feudatories), which were subdivided into Parganas administered by a Sikdar. These in turn comprised Garkhs or Pattis managed by a Negi, with villages at the lowest level headed by a Sayana or Boodha.

### State Employees

King Lakshmi Chand organized state employees into three tiers: Sardars who ruled a Pargana, Faujdars who served as military officers, and Negis who collected customary dues in grain.

### Village Administration

At the village level, the Sayana or Boodha served as the village head and also functioned as a local judge. The Kotal maintained revenue accounts, and the Prahari served as the village watchman. The village panchayat was known as the Boodhachoura.

### Justice System

Lakshmi Chand reformed the justice system by establishing two formal courts: the Nyovaali for civil and public cases, and the Bishtaali for military cases. Punishments included death by hanging from a tree, mutilation, exile, and confiscation of property.

## 3.5 ECONOMY, LAND POLICY & THE 36 TAXES (CHHATISI)

### Land Ownership Hierarchy

The land ownership structure comprised four tiers. The King was the supreme landowner. Beneath him, Thatwans served as the primary landholders. Khaikars were tenant farmers who cultivated under the Thatwans. At the lowest level, Sirtans were sharecroppers who paid annual rent.

### Land Grant Types

Two primary categories of land grants existed. Raut grants were awarded for bravery in war, while Goonth grants provided tax-free land for Brahmins, temples, and pilgrims.

### Revenue Rate

The standard land revenue was known as *Galla-Chhahada* — fixed at one-sixth of agricultural produce. This was notably lighter than the Parmar tax (Tihar) of one-third of produce, but the Chands compensated for this lower direct tax with their extensive system of 36 indirect taxes.

### The 36 Taxes — CHHATISI

The Chand taxation system was codified into 36 distinct levies, collectively known as the Chhatisi. The most significant of these included:

| Tax Name | Description |
|---|---|
| **Sirtan / Sirti** | Primary cash tax (later imposed specifically on Bhotiyas by Baj Bahadur Chand) |
| **Koot / Khoot** | Tax payable strictly in grain |
| **Katak** | Dedicated tax collected for the army |
| **Jyuliya / Sanga** | Toll tax at river bridges |
| **Kheni Kapilni** | Forced unpaid labour (Begar) |
| **Rakhiya** | Tax during Raksha Bandhan and Janeu ceremonies |
| **Mijhari** | Tax on labourers and Jagaris |
| **Tika Bhent** | Levied on marriages and auspicious occasions |
| **Maro Kar** | Tax on a person who died without a son |
| **Sonya Phagun** | Tax during festivals |

## 3.6 CHAND SOCIETY & CULTURE

### Social Structure

Chand society was formally rigidified by Rudra Chand's *Traivarnik Dharmanirnay*, which divided Brahmins into strict sub-castes (Chauthani, Tithani, Pitaliye). A unique social development was the genesis of the Nayak (Katwal) Caste, born from relationships between Chand soldiers and Doti women during Bharti Chand's twelve-year war.

### Art & Architecture

The Chand period produced remarkable achievements in stone carving, with the Baleshwar Temple Group at Champawat representing the masterpiece of Chand art. Water conservation was integrated into temple complexes through the construction of Naulas (covered step-wells or springs), such as the Ek Hathiya Ka Naula at Champawat. The Hathiya Dewal Temple at Thal, Pithoragarh, was modeled after the Ellora caves, and Lakshmi Chand renovated the Bagnath Temple at Bageshwar.

## 3.7 DECLINE & FALL OF THE CHAND DYNASTY

The decline of the Chand Dynasty was driven by multiple factors. Weak later rulers crippled the dynasty's capacity. Internal conspiracies saw court factions fighting for power. External Rohilla attacks weakened the state. The fatal blow came through the betrayal of Diwan Harshdev Joshi — known as both the 'Chanakya of Kumaon' and the 'Earl of Warwick' — who invited the Gorkhas to attack. The dynasty met its end at the Battle of Hawalabagh in 1790 CE, where the last king, Mahendra Chand, was defeated by Gorkha forces.

Harshdev Joshi's manipulation stands as the single most important human factor in the fall of both the Chand Dynasty (1790) and the Parmar Dynasty (1804), as he invited and assisted the Gorkhas in both invasions.

## PART B: THE GORKHA RULE IN UTTARAKHAND (1790–1815)

Historically termed 'Gorkhyali', this period is remembered as a dark era of tyrannical and atrocities-based military administration.

## 3.8 THE GORKHA INVASION — TIMELINE

| Year | Event | Key Details |
|---|---|---|
| **1790** | Gorkhas conquer Kumaon | Led by Hastidal Chautariya, Amar Singh Thapa, Jagjit Pandey; defeated last Chand King Mahendra Chand at Hawalabag field |
| **1791** | First attack on Garhwal | Battle of Langurgarh — settled for annual tribute of Rs 25,000 from Pradyumna Shah |
| **1803** | Devastating earthquake | September 1, 1803 — 9.0 magnitude; destroyed Garhwal's infrastructure |
| **1804** | Gorkhas conquer Garhwal | Pradyumna Shah martyred at Battle of Khurbura (May 14, 1804) |
| **1814–15** | Anglo-Gorkha War | British defeat Gorkhas |
| **1815** | Gorkha rule ends | Uttarakhand liberated |
| **1816** | Treaty of Sugauli | Ratified by Nepal (May 4, 1816); formally ended conflict |

The duration of Gorkha rule varied by region: 25 years in Kumaon (1790–1815) and 10.5 years in Garhwal (1804–1815).

## 3.9 GORKHA MILITARY & CIVIL ADMINISTRATION

The King of Nepal served as the supreme overlord, with conquered territories administered by a feudal military governor known as the Subba or Subedar.

### Key Gorkha Subedars

| Subedar | Period | Key Actions |
|---|---|---|
| **Jogamal Shah** | 1791–1792 | First Gorkha Subedar of Kumaon |
| **Amar Singh Thapa** | — | Powerful commander; titled Kazi; established Ganga Mata Temple at Gangotri |
| **Ranjor Thapa** | 1804–1805 | Created posts of Vichari (Judge) and Avichari (Administrator); patronized painter Mola Ram — gave him title 'Danveer Karna' |
| **Hastidal Chautariya** | 1805–1808 | Unlike other cruel rulers — showed concern for farmers; provided Taqavi (agricultural) loans |

### Military Structure

The Gorkha military comprised two components: the Jagcha (Jagariya), which formed the permanent army, and the Dhakariya, a temporary force. Soldiers received Rs 8 per month during wartime and Rs 6 per month during peacetime. The primary weapon was the Khukhri.

## 3.10 THE BRUTAL JUSTICE SYSTEM — DIVYA PARIKSHA (ORDEALS)

The Gorkhas had no fixed, codified laws. Justice was arbitrary and based on extreme physical ordeals known as *Dip*. The first formal court was established in Almora, headed by a Vichari (judge).

### Types of Ordeals (Dip)

Three principal ordeals determined guilt or innocence. In Goladip, the accused was required to carry a red-hot iron rod in bare hands for a fixed distance. Tarajudip involved the accused being weighed at night on a scale — if lighter the next morning, they were deemed innocent; if heavier, guilty. In the most terrifying ordeal, Kadhai Dip, the accused was forced to plunge their hands into a cauldron of boiling oil.

### Punishment System

Treason was punished by death. Murder brought hanging from a tree. General crimes were punished by mutilation — typically the cutting off of hands or noses. Brahmin offenders were exempt from the death penalty and instead punished by exile or cutting off their *shikha* (tuft of hair). This caste-based judicial inequality reinforced social hierarchy even within the penal system.

## 3.11 THE OPPRESSIVE TAXATION SYSTEM

| Tax Name | Description |
|---|---|
| **Pungdi Kar** | Primary land tax |
| **Kushahi Kar** | Specific tax on Brahmins for land acquisition |
| **Manga Kar** | Tax collected specifically during wartime |
| **Tika Bhent Kar** | Levied on marriages and auspicious occasions |
| **Mijhari Kar** | Levied on Jagaris and Brahmins |
| **Maro Kar** | Tax on a person who died without leaving a son |
| **Timari Kar** | Tax given directly to Gorkha Faujdars and Subedars |
| **Sonya Phagun Kar** | Tax levied during festivals |
| **Rakhiya** | Tax during religious ceremonies |

Both the Chand Chhatisi and the Gorkha tax systems were exploitative, but Gorkha taxes were more arbitrary and specifically linked to life events such as death, marriage, and festivals.

## 3.12 SOCIAL & CULTURAL IMPACT OF GORKHA RULE

### New Social Categories Imposed by Gorkhas

The Gorkhas imposed new social designations on the local population. Kami denoted local craftsmen, Sunuwar designated goldsmiths, Nau referred to barbers, and most significantly, Kathua formalized an entire slave class.

### Cultural Destruction

The Divya Pariksha system replaced rational justice with terror and superstition. Cultural celebrations were suffocated by the Sonya Phagun Kar and Tika Bhent Kar taxes levied on festivals and auspicious occasions. The begar system of forced labour was intensified beyond the limits that had existed under the Chands. The Gorkha penal code deepened social inequality through its caste-based punishments.

### The Most Profound Impact

The Gorkha tyranny was so severe that when the British took control in 1815, their administration appeared "liberal and reformist" in comparison. The traumatized population welcomed British rule without immediate mass resistance — this psychological conditioning is the most significant long-term consequence of the Gorkhyali period.

## 3.13 THE ANGLO-GORKHA WAR & LIBERATION (1814–1815)

The war was precipitated when Gorkha expansionism clashed with British East India Company interests. The British Governor-General declared war in 1814.

### The Battle of Nalapani (Khalanga Fort, Dehradun)

The most celebrated engagement of the war took place at Nalapani (Khalanga) Fort in Dehradun. British forces under Major General Gillespie assaulted the fort, which was defended by Captain Balbhadra Thapa with just 500 Gorkha soldiers. Gillespie was killed in battle on October 31, 1814. The British ultimately prevailed only by cutting off the fort's drinking water supply. In tribute to the extraordinary bravery displayed on both sides, the British built the Khalanga War Memorial in Dehradun — unique in military history for honouring both the victors and the vanquished.

### Treaty of Sugauli

The Treaty of Sugauli was signed between the British and Nepal, ratified by Nepal on May 4, 1816. It formally ended the conflict and liberated Uttarakhand from Gorkha rule. In the post-war division, Eastern Garhwal (Pauri) was retained by the British, while Western Garhwal was returned to Sudarshan Shah as the Tehri State.

### COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS — KEY INSIGHTS

**Political Consolidation:** The Katyuris were the first to unify the region under one banner. The Parmars under Ajaypal unified 52 Garhs to create the 'Garhwal' identity. The Chands freed Kumaon from Nepal and centralized at Almora. The Gorkhas destroyed both identities and imposed foreign military rule.

**Economic Systems — Progressive to Regressive:** The trajectory moved from the Katyuri basic agrarian system, through the Parmar system (one-third tax plus land grants), to the Chand system (one-sixth tax but with 36 codified taxes), ending with the Gorkha regime of arbitrary extortion.

**Justice Evolution:** The system evolved from the Katyuri era where the King was supreme, through the Parmar system of Panchayats and a Diwan's court, to the Chand system's two formal courts — the most advanced of all — before regressing completely under the Gorkhas to a system of ordeals and torture.

**Cultural Contribution:** Each dynasty left a distinct cultural legacy. The Katyuris contributed magnificent temples at Jageshwar and Dwarahat. The Parmars produced the Garhwal School of Painting. The Chands excelled in stone carving and water architecture (Naulas). The Gorkhas contributed nothing but cultural destruction.
