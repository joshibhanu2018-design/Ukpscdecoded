## 25.1 NATURE AND TYPOLOGY OF DISASTERS IN UTTARAKHAND

Uttarakhand's young, unstable geological formations shaped by the ongoing Himalayan orogeny, combined with heavy monsoon dynamics and mounting anthropogenic pressures, expose the state to diverse natural hazards. Situated on active tectonic zones, receiving intense monsoonal rainfall, and facing growing developmental pressures, Uttarakhand ranks among India's most disaster-prone regions.

### Master Disaster Classification Table

| Disaster Type | Nature / Mechanism | Vulnerable Zones | Recent Major Event (2025–26) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Earthquake** | Tectonic strain release between Indian & Eurasian plates | Entire state (Zone IV & V); Central Seismic Gap | Constant micro-tremors (2025–26); high risk of Magnitude 8+ event |
| **Landslide** | Slope failure due to gravity, rain, toe-cutting of hills | MCT & MBT belts; Chamoli, Rudraprayag, Uttarkashi | July 2026: 111 roads blocked state-wide |
| **Cloudburst (Badal Fatna)** | Extreme rainfall >100 mm/hr in small area; orographic lifting | Higher Himalayas: Rudraprayag, Uttarkashi, Chamoli | Aug 5, 2025: Dharali flash flood (61+ dead) |
| **Forest Fire (Vanalanal)** | Dry pine needle ignition during April–June | Pine belts: Almora, Nainital, Pauri, Tehri | Summer 2026: 1,000+ incidents; 300% spike |
| **GLOF** | Breach of unstable moraine-dammed glacial lakes | 500+ glacial lakes in High Himalayas | 2026 study: 500 expanding lakes identified |
| **Land Subsidence** | Ground sinking due to aquifer drainage/unstable debris | Joshimath, Nainital, Mussoorie | Joshimath: ₹1,658 Cr R&R Plan approved |
| **Avalanche** | Rapid flow of snow/ice/rock down high-altitude slopes | Gangotri, Kedarnath, Nanda Devi massifs (>3500m) | Oct 2022: Draupadi Ka Danda II tragedy |
| **Drought** | Erratic snowfall (Western Disturbances) & weak monsoons | Agricultural zones; spring-fed areas | 2025–26: Historic "Snow Drought" |


### A. Earthquakes

Earthquakes in Uttarakhand are caused by the tectonic convergence between the Indian and Eurasian plates, which builds immense sub-surface strain. The entire state falls within Seismic Zone IV (Severe) and Zone V (Very Severe). The most critical concept for understanding the state's seismic vulnerability is the Central Himalayan Seismic Gap — a region where historical stress accumulation suggests no major release has occurred in centuries, indicating a high probability of a major or great magnitude earthquake capable of reaching 8+ on the Richter scale.

### B. Landslides

Landslides are triggered by a combination of steep slopes, weak rock strata, heavy rainfall, and anthropogenic factors such as toe-cutting for road widening and unscientific excavation. These events frequently sever vital pilgrimage and strategic routes. The most vulnerable geological zones are along the Main Central Thrust (MCT) and Main Boundary Thrust (MBT) belts. During the July 2026 "Monsoon Fury," landslides blocked 111 roads state-wide.

### C. Cloudbursts (Badal Fatna)

A cloudburst is defined as extreme localized precipitation exceeding 100 mm per hour over a small geographical area. The mechanism involves rapid orographic lifting of warm, moisture-laden monsoon winds interacting with the steep Himalayan topography, resulting in intense flash floods with debris flows. The higher Himalayan valleys of Rudraprayag, Uttarkashi, and Chamoli are particularly vulnerable to these events.

### D. Forest Fires (Vanalanal)

Forest fires peak during the dry summer months from April to June. The primary fuel is the dry, highly flammable Chir Pine needle (Pirul), with aggravating factors including the invasive weed Lantana camara and intentional burning for grazing. These fires heavily damage biodiversity, destroy carbon sinks, and choke regional air quality. The summer of 2026 witnessed a historic 300% spike in fire incidents due to the preceding winter's "Snow Drought."

### E. Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs)

GLOFs are caused by rapid glacier melting due to global warming, which forms unstable moraine-dammed lakes. When these moraine dams breach suddenly, colossal surges of water, silt, and boulders rush down river valleys. A 2026 study identified over 500 glacial lakes in Uttarakhand's High Himalayas, with monitoring stations being established at the 15 most dangerous sites.


### F. Droughts and Meteorological Anomalies

Droughts in Uttarakhand are caused by increasingly erratic winter snowfalls from Western Disturbances and weak monsoons. Their impact manifests as agricultural drought and the drying up of perennial natural springs known as Naulas and Dharas. The 2025–26 period witnessed a historic "Snow Drought" that left soils exceptionally dry, directly fueling the massive forest fire surge that followed.

### G. Avalanches

Avalanches involve sudden sweeping masses of snow, ice, and rock tumbling down high-altitude slopes. They threaten mountaineering expeditions, border military posts, and high-altitude hydroelectric projects. The most notable recent event was the October 2022 Draupadi Ka Danda II tragedy, in which NIM trainees were killed.

## 25.2 MAJOR LANDMARK DISASTER EVENTS

### A. The 2013 Kedarnath Disaster (The Himalayan Tsunami)

On June 16–17, 2013, a catastrophic disaster struck Kedarnath in Rudraprayag District. A historic multi-day cloudburst combined with the breach of Chorabari Tal, a glacial lake situated above the Kedarnath shrine, triggered a devastating Glacial Lake Outburst Flood. The GLOF merged with the swollen Mandakini and Saraswati rivers, creating massive debris flows that wiped out Kedarnath town's surrounding infrastructure, claimed over 4,000 lives, stranded tens of thousands of pilgrims, and crippled the region's tourism-dependent economy. Notably, the main Kedarnath shrine miraculously survived the deluge. The 2013 Kedarnath disaster remains the benchmark disaster for Uttarakhand's disaster policy, fundamentally transforming the state's approach from reactive relief to proactive technology-driven preparedness. It accelerated the deployment of state disaster response mechanisms, strengthened scientific monitoring of upper catchment zones, and provided impetus for the Char Dham All-Weather Road project.

### B. The 2021 Chamoli Disaster (Raini / Rishiganga Flood)

On February 7, 2021, a massive rock-ice avalanche broke off from the Nanda Ghunti/Trisul area in Chamoli District and plunged into the Rishiganga river, generating a destructive debris-laden flash flood that swept through the Rishiganga and Dhauliganga valleys. The flood completely destroyed the 24 MW Rishiganga hydroelectric project and heavily damaged the 520 MW Tapovan-Vishnugad project, killing approximately 200 people — primarily project workers and local laborers. This disaster highlighted the extreme vulnerability of high-altitude run-of-the-river hydroelectric projects to cryospheric hazards driven by climate change, and intensified the national debate over hydropower development in the Higher Himalayas.


### C. The 2025 Dharali Flash Flood Disaster

On August 5, 2025, a catastrophic flash flood struck the Dharali and Harsil areas in Uttarkashi District along the Gangotri Dham pilgrim route. The event is suspected to have been a "Compound Event" — a cloudburst combined with the breach of a high-altitude hanging glacier or small glacial lake upstream along the Kheer Ganga (Kheer Gad) river. A wall of mud and boulders descended rapidly, killing 61 people with 84 declared missing. The flood washed away 40–50 houses, submerged Dharali market, destroyed dozens of hotels and homestays, swept away the ancient Kalp Kedar temple, trapped military personnel and infrastructure near Harsil, and severed the Gangotri Highway for weeks. ISRO Cartosat-2S satellite imagery subsequently mapped massive fan-shaped debris deposits covering approximately 20 hectares, choking the confluence with the Bhagirathi River. As of 2026, reconstruction is ongoing and new "danger zones" have been marked along the river banks.

### D. The Silkyara-Barkot Tunnel Collapse (November 2023)

In November 2023, part of a 4.5 km tunnel on the Yamunotri National Highway near Silkyara in Uttarkashi collapsed, trapping 41 workers for 17 days. The collapse occurred in a "shear zone" — a stretch of highly fractured, unstable rock that had not been adequately reinforced. Additionally, no independent escape tunnel had been built, despite this being standard international practice for long tunnels. The rescue operation became a national spectacle when US-made Auger machines failed and traditional "rat-hole miners" were ultimately brought in to complete the extraction. The policy impact was significant: a new mandate now requires all tunnels exceeding 1.5 km to have parallel escape tunnels.

## 25.3 SEISMIC ZONES IN UTTARAKHAND

Uttarakhand is located in one of the most seismically active regions globally, sitting squarely across the active tectonic zone where the Indian Plate continuously thrusts beneath the Eurasian Plate.

### Seismic Zone Classification

| Zone | Intensity | Districts Covered | Risk Profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Zone V** (Very Severe) | Magnitude 7.0+ | Entire districts: Pithoragarh, Bageshwar, Chamoli, Rudraprayag; Northern parts: Uttarkashi, Tehri Garhwal, Almora, Champawat | Prone to earthquakes of Magnitude 8+; sits directly on active fault lines |
| **Zone IV** (Severe) | Magnitude 6.0–7.0 | Dehradun, Haridwar, Udham Singh Nagar, Nainital; Southern parts: Pauri Garhwal, Tehri | Severe threat from ground shaking and liquefaction, especially in sediment-heavy plains |


### The Central Himalayan Seismic Gap

The Central Himalayan Seismic Gap is an area where historical stress accumulation suggests no major energy release has occurred in centuries. Located along a significant portion of Uttarakhand's underground arc, this feature implies a high probability of a major or great magnitude earthquake in the future. The scientific basis rests on the fact that tectonic strain builds continuously, and the absence of periodic release means energy is "locked" beneath the surface. Constant micro-tremors reported in 2025–26 have led seismologists to warn of elevated risk. The entire state falls in Zone IV and V — there is no low-risk zone in Uttarakhand. The Central Himalayan Seismic Gap is considered one of the most dangerous geological features in India, with potential for a Magnitude 8+ "Great Earthquake" that has not occurred in recorded history.

### Key Tectonic Lines

| Tectonic Feature | Location | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Main Central Thrust (MCT)** | Passes near Joshimath, Kedarnath, Munsiyari | Most active earthquake/landslide zone |
| **Main Boundary Thrust (MBT)** | Passes near Nainital, Mussoorie | High landslide risk |

## 25.4 ECOLOGICALLY SENSITIVE AREAS (ESAs) AND CONSERVATION FRAMEWORKS

Given its fragile topography and rich biodiversity, parts of Uttarakhand are protected under strict environmental governance regimes notified by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC).

### A. Bhagirathi Eco-Sensitive Zone (BESZ)

The Bhagirathi Eco-Sensitive Zone covers 4,179.59 sq. km along a 100 km stretch from Gaumukh to Uttarkashi town along the Bhagirathi river. Key restrictions include a total ban on new hydropower projects exceeding 2 MW, prohibition of riverbed mining, and a ban on polluting industries classified as Red Category. This is the only stretch of the Ganga allowed to flow in its pristine, free-flowing form. The BESZ represents a landmark governance framework regulating commercial tourism and protecting the upper Himalayan ecosystem.

### B. Doon Valley Eco-Sensitive Zone

Notified in 1989, the Doon Valley ESZ was one of India's first Ecologically Sensitive Zones. Key restrictions include a ban on limestone quarrying and mining, along with strict limits on land-use changes from Green/Agriculture to Commercial/Industrial categories. All major constructions require clearance from the Doon Valley Notification Committee.


### C. Protected Area ESZs (Buffer Zones)

Every National Park and Wildlife Sanctuary has a notified ESZ buffer (usually 1–10 km) where commercial activities are regulated.

| Protected Area | ESZ Regulation |
|---|---|
| **Jim Corbett** | Regulates resort density in Ramnagar/Mohan areas |
| **Rajaji** | Buffer zone restrictions on commercial development |
| **Gangotri** | Strict regulation of tourism and construction |
| **Govind Pashu Vihar** | Controls mining and industrial activity |
| **Nanda Devi Biosphere Reserve** | Strict no-entry core zone; regulated trekking in buffer |

### D. Gross Environment Product (GEP)

Uttarakhand has pioneered the Gross Environment Product as an administrative tool that quantifies ecological health by measuring services rendered by forests, water bodies, soil, and clean air. The goal is to balance economic growth with environmental preservation by providing decision-makers with data on the monetary value of natural ecosystems.

## 25.5 ADVANCED EARLY WARNING SYSTEMS (EWS) AND TECHNOLOGY INTERVENTIONS

Uttarakhand has moved from a "reactionary" relief model to a "technology-driven" prediction and prevention system. Modern technology pipelines are deployed to mitigate loss of life from rapid-onset hydro-meteorological and seismic hazards.

### Summary Table: Early Warning Systems

| System | Technology & Function | Status (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| **Bhudev App** | Earthquake EWS: Sensors detect P-waves and send siren alert 10–40 seconds before destructive S-waves | Operational; expanding sensor network (170+ sensors) |
| **Doppler Weather Radars** | Detects cloud density and wind moisture to predict cloudbursts 30–60 minutes in advance | Operational at Surkanda Devi, Mukteshwar, Lansdowne; new radars approved for Haridwar, Pantnagar, Auli |
| **Siren Towers** | Flash Flood: Automated river-gauge sensors trigger loud sirens in riverbank towns | Installed in Kedarnath, Rishikesh, Haldwani |
| **SILAAS** | Satellite Integrated Landslide Assessment and Alert System | Operational via USAC |
| **FSI Fire Alerts** | SNPP-VIIRS/MODIS satellites detect heat anomalies; SMS alerts within 45 minutes | Highly active during summer 2026 |
| **Cell Broadcast EWS** | Forces loud siren on all mobile phones in specific tower radius (even on silent mode) | Tested in 2026 |
| **InSAR Monitoring** | Satellite radar tracks millimeter-level ground movement on highways | Piloted by MoRTH on NH-58 |


### A. Earthquake Early Warning System (EEWS) — "Bhudev" App

The Bhudev App was developed jointly by USDMA and IIT Roorkee as Uttarakhand's Earthquake Early Warning System. It operates through a network of 170+ subsurface strong-motion accelerometers deployed across high-risk seismic zones. The system works by detecting primary waves (P-waves), which travel faster but cause no damage, before the destructive secondary waves (S-waves) arrive. P-waves travel faster but cause no damage and are detected by sensors first, while S-waves travel slower but cause destructive shaking and arrive seconds later. The system exploits this time gap to warn citizens before the destructive shaking begins. Even 10 seconds of lead time can save lives by allowing people to move to safe zones. The official "Uttarakhand Bhudev App" broadcasts automated audio-visual warnings directly to smartphones with a lead time of 10–40 seconds depending on epicenter distance. It sends alerts for damaging earthquakes above 5.0 magnitude and produces sirens loudly even on silent mode while sending GPS location data to rescue teams for rapid search-and-rescue dispatch.

### B. Doppler Weather Radars

Deployed by the India Meteorological Department (IMD) in coordination with state authorities, Doppler Weather Radars provide high-resolution, real-time spatial tracking of clouds, severe storms, cloudburst triggers, and heavy downpours. They can predict cloudbursts 30–60 minutes in advance. Operational locations include Surkanda Devi (Tehri), Mukteshwar (Nainital), and Lansdowne (Pauri), with new installations approved for Haridwar, Pantnagar, and Auli during 2025–26 to cover the Nanda Devi shadow zone. The output feeds critical inputs into flash-flood early warning models.

### C. Automated Weather Stations (AWS) and Real-Time River Gauges

A dense telemetry-based network of Automatic Weather Stations (AWS) and Automatic Rain Gauges (ARGs) is deployed along river catchments, linked directly to central disaster control rooms. These stations function by sounding automated sirens downstream when sudden water level surges or glacial lake breaches are detected. Flash flood sirens connected to automated river-gauge sensors trigger automatically to clear ghats if water levels rise rapidly due to dam releases or upstream cloudbursts. These systems are installed along river banks in Kedarnath, Rishikesh, and Haldwani.


### D. Landslide Warning: SILAAS and FLIM App

The Satellite Integrated Landslide Assessment and Alert System (SILAAS) is operated by the Uttarakhand Space Application Centre (USAC). It uses satellite data combined with ground verification to generate "Landslide Hazard Maps" during heavy rains. The accompanying FLIM App is used by ground staff to upload real-time photos of cracking roads to update the central server.

### E. Forest Fire Detection: FSI Alert System

The Forest Survey of India's fire detection system uses SNPP-VIIRS and MODIS satellite sensors to detect "heat anomalies" — temperatures exceeding 40°C in forest grids. The Van Agni Geo-Portal automates SMS alerts to registered Beat Officers' phones within 45 minutes of fire detection. The system was highly active during the record fire season of 2026.

## 25.6 CHRONIC ANTHROPOGENIC (MAN-MADE) DISASTERS

Anthropogenic disasters are man-made crises caused by engineering failures, unplanned urbanization, or ecological neglect. In Uttarakhand, these are often "Compound Disasters" — where human intervention destabilizes fragile Himalayan slopes, turning natural weather variations into destructive events.

### A. The Joshimath Sinking Crisis (Land Subsidence)

Joshimath — the holy town that serves as the strategic gateway to Badrinath and Auli — is built on an ancient landslide-prone moraine deposit with low load-bearing capacity. The town has experienced catastrophic land subsidence, with wide structural cracks appearing across 800+ houses, roads, hotels, and land parcels. The anthropogenic drivers include unregulated multi-story construction overloading loose soil, the absence of proper sewerage and drainage allowing wastewater to soak into the ground and turn soil into fluid slurry, and cumulative stress from hydroelectric tunneling (the Tapovan-Vishnugad project breached a major aquifer in 2009). The management response included immediate evacuation, expert committees from IITs, Wadia Institute, and ISRO, safety mapping dividing the town into Danger, Buffer, and Safe zones, and a ₹1,658 Crore R&R Plan. Red Zone residents have been permanently resettled to Gauchar and Pipalkoti, while Yellow Zone houses have been retrofitted with stronger foundations. As of 2026, the town remains in a "stabilization and retreat" phase with subsidence still active in pockets.


### B. Unchecked Hydropower Projects (Chamoli 2021)

High-altitude rock-ice avalanches triggered catastrophic debris flows in Chamoli District, completely wiping out the Rishi Ganga project (24 MW) and heavily damaging the Tapovan-Vishnugad project (520 MW), killing approximately 200 people. The key anthropogenic driver was the proliferation of run-of-the-river hydro projects in narrow, volatile gorges without adequate cumulative ecological impact assessments.

### C. Silkyara-Barkot Tunnel Collapse (November 2023)

Part of the Char Dham road-widening mega project, the Silkyara tunnel collapse trapped 41 workers for 17 days. The anthropogenic drivers included "Shear Zone Ignorance" — the tunnel crossed highly fractured unstable rock without adequate reinforcement — and the failure to build an independent escape tunnel, which is standard international practice.

### D. Forest Fire Surge and Pine Monoculture

Summer fire seasons now routinely burn thousands of hectares in Uttarakhand. The anthropogenic drivers include the colonial-era replacement of fire-resistant Oak forests with commercial Chir Pine, whose needles (Pirul) contain highly flammable resin that forms thick blankets on the forest floor, and the intentional setting of fires by locals for grazing stimulation. The 2026 season saw a record surge of over 1,000 incidents in January–April due to the preceding "Snow Drought."

### E. Char Dham Highway Slurry Disasters (Ongoing)

The 900 km road-widening project meant to improve pilgrimage transit has triggered a massive wave of active landslide zones. The anthropogenic drivers include vertical slope cutting at 90-degree angles instead of terraced slopes, and the dumping of millions of tons of excavated "muck" directly into the Alaknanda and Bhagirathi rivers, which raises riverbeds and causes downstream flash floods.

### Underlying Causes of Man-Made Disasters

The root causes of anthropogenic disasters in Uttarakhand include unregulated urbanization and tourism pressure (unscientific real estate booms, encroachment on river floodplains, and violation of local carrying capacities), heavy infrastructure blasting (high-intensity explosives for road widening and tunneling that fracture unstable phyllite and quartzite formations), disruption of natural drainage (blockage of springs, rivulets, and sub-surface channels by debris dumping and concrete expansion, allowing rainwater to seep into vulnerable slopes), and a systemic lack of geological surveys before projects proceed.


## 25.7 THE DISASTER MANAGEMENT ACT, 2005

Enacted in response to systemic gaps exposed by mega-disasters (the 1999 Odisha Cyclone and the 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami), the Disaster Management Act, 2005 provides the overarching legal and institutional architecture for disaster governance in India. The Act represented a paradigm shift from reactive, post-disaster relief and rehabilitation to proactive prevention, mitigation, preparedness, and capacity-building. It replaced ad-hoc response mechanisms with a seamless multi-tiered statutory hierarchy, created the specialized NDRF by statute, and established a two-tier financial architecture comprising both Response and Mitigation funds.

### Multi-Tiered Statutory Structure

The Act establishes a three-tier institutional framework. At the National Level, it creates the NDMA (National Disaster Management Authority) chaired by the Prime Minister, the NEC (National Executive Committee) headed by the Union Home Secretary, and the NDRF (National Disaster Response Force). At the State Level, it mandates the SDMA (State Disaster Management Authority) chaired by the Chief Minister, the SEC (State Executive Committee) headed by the Chief Secretary, and the SDRF (State Disaster Response Force). At the District Level, it establishes the DDMA (District Disaster Management Authority), co-chaired by the District Magistrate and the Zila Parishad Chairperson.

### Key Statutory Provisions

| Section | Provision | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Section 3** | Establishment of NDMA | Creates apex national body |
| **Section 14** | Every state must establish SDMA | Mandates state-level institutionalization |
| **Section 25** | District authorities to establish DDMA | Ensures ground-level coordination |
| **Section 33** | Emergency Procurement | Allows bypassing standard tendering during emergencies for quick acquisition of relief materials |
| **Section 44** | Establishment of NDRF | Creates specialized response force |
| **Sections 51–60** | Penal Provisions | Fines and imprisonment for obstructing rescue, raising false alarms, or misappropriating relief funds |

### Two-Tier Financial Architecture

| Fund Type | National Level | State Level | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Response Fund** | National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) | State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) | Immediate rescue and relief works |
| **Mitigation Fund** | National Disaster Mitigation Fund (NDMF) | State Disaster Mitigation Fund (SDMF) | Long-term risk-reduction projects (slope stabilization, earthquake-resistant buildings) |


## 25.8 NATIONAL DISASTER MANAGEMENT AUTHORITY (NDMA)

### Structure and Composition

The NDMA is the supreme statutory apex body for disaster management in India, established under Section 3 of the Disaster Management Act 2005. It is chaired by the Prime Minister of India (ex-officio) and comprises up to nine members nominated by the Chairperson, with one member designated as Vice-Chairperson. Members are drawn from expert fields including administrative management, meteorology, structural engineering, and medical preparedness.

### Core Responsibilities

- Lays down national policies, plans, and guidelines for disaster management
- Approves the National Disaster Management Plan
- Coordinates enforcement and implementation of mitigation strategies across central ministries
- Authorizes and allocates funds from the National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF)

### National Executive Committee (NEC)

The NEC is headed by the Union Home Secretary and serves as the coordinating executive arm that assists the NDMA. It executes national plans and coordinates inter-ministerial responses during disasters.

## 25.9 STATE DISASTER MANAGEMENT AUTHORITY (SDMA) — UTTARAKHAND

### Structure and Composition (USDMA)

The Uttarakhand SDMA is established under Section 14 of the DM Act 2005 and is chaired by the Chief Minister of Uttarakhand (ex-officio). It comprises up to 8 other members nominated by the Chairperson, with the Chief Secretary serving as CEO and heading the State Executive Committee (SEC).

### Key Functions of USDMA

The USDMA approves and reviews the State Disaster Management Plan, reviews risk profiles of all 13 districts (seismic, landslide, flood), oversees implementation of technology systems including the Bhudev App, Doppler radars, and SILAAS, issues guidelines for disaster prevention and mitigation, administers the State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) for immediate relief and ex-gratia compensation, and integrates disaster mitigation norms into regional infrastructure and hill-development blueprints.

### Operational Mechanisms

The State Emergency Operation Centre (SEOC) operates 24×7 for real-time monitoring and is accessible via Helpline 1070. District Emergency Operation Centres (DEOCs) coordinate at the district level via Helpline 1077. Annual pre-monsoon review meetings headed by the Chief Secretary and Chief Minister mandate safety audits of bridges, pilgrim footfall tracking, and carrying-capacity monitoring.


## 25.10 ROLE OF NDRF AND SDRF

### Comparative Framework

| Parameter | NDRF (National) | SDRF (Uttarakhand) |
|---|---|---|
| **Established Under** | Section 44, DM Act 2005 | State Government (modeled on national framework) |
| **Administrative Control** | Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA) | State Government (Uttarakhand State Police) |
| **Nature** | Central, paramilitary-trained force | State-level force raised from state police |
| **Deployment** | Macro/severe crises requiring heavy machinery, specialized airlift | Immediate first responder; localized rapid response |
| **Response Time** | Deployed dynamically during peaks (pre-positioned near Char Dham, river valleys) | Significantly faster — stationed locally across vulnerable mountain sectors |
| **Key Strengths** | Advanced structural collapse extraction; aquatic disaster response; CBRN capabilities | High-altitude mountaineering rescue; swift-river maneuvering; localized fire containment |
| **Key Deployments** | 2023 Silkyara Tunnel (technical drilling); 2021 Chamoli (large-scale debris clearance) | 2025 Dharali flash flood; seasonal cloudburst rescue; Char Dham pilgrim rescue |
| **Equipment** | Cutting-edge heavy-rescue tools, K-9 squads, deep-diving equipment, CBRN kits | Mountain rescue gear, river recovery equipment, vertical cliff rescue systems |

### A. National Disaster Response Force (NDRF)

The NDRF was established under Section 44 of the DM Act 2005 under the Ministry of Home Affairs. Its battalions are pre-positioned across strategic high-risk zones nationwide. The force possesses cutting-edge heavy-rescue tools, canine squads (K-9), deep-diving equipment, and CBRN response kits. In Uttarakhand, the NDRF is deployed dynamically near major pilgrimage corridors (Char Dham tracks) and high-risk river valleys during monsoon peaks. Key operations in the state include leading technical drilling during the 2023 Silkyara Tunnel rescue and managing large-scale debris clearances during the 2021 Chamoli floods.

### B. State Disaster Response Force (SDRF — Uttarakhand)

The SDRF is the state's premier specialized tactical unit and serves as the first responder in disaster situations. Raised from the Uttarakhand State Police, the force is decentralized and stationed at vulnerable nodes across districts including Rudraprayag, Chamoli, Uttarkashi, and Pithoragarh. Its response time is within minutes of disaster reporting, bridging the gap before central reinforcements arrive. The SDRF's specialized skills include vertical cliff rescue, swift-water river rafting recovery, deep-crevice extraction, and snow-avalanche rescue. Key operations include the 2025 Dharali event, seasonal pilgrim rescue on high-altitude Char Dham tracks, and localized forest fire containment. In essence, the SDRF functions as the first responder (local, fast, terrain-expert) while the NDRF serves as heavy reinforcement (central, specialized, for macro-crises).


## 25.11 GOVERNMENT MITIGATION INITIATIVES AND INSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORK

The government's disaster mitigation strategy has shifted from a "relief-centric" approach to a "technology-first" and "nature-based" defense system.

### A. Institutional Architecture

| Institution | Role | Key Details |
|---|---|---|
| **USDMA** | Apex state body | Chaired by CM; frames policy, coordinates execution |
| **SDRF** | First responders | Specialized units at vulnerable nodes |
| **SEOC / DEOC** | 24×7 command network | Helplines: 1070 (SEOC), 1077 (DEOC) |
| **SARRA** | Spring & River Rejuvenation Authority | Established Nov 2023; by July 2026 treated 929 critical water sources to recharge groundwater |
| **ULMMC** | Uttarakhand Landslide Mitigation & Management Centre | Autonomous Centre of Excellence; mandates "Geotechnical Audits" for all major infrastructure projects |

### B. World Bank Projects and Infrastructure Resilience

#### U-PREPARE (Uttarakhand Disaster Resilience and Preparedness Project)

The U-PREPARE initiative, backed by World Bank funding, encompasses multiple components for building disaster resilience. Its Road and Bridge Resilience component reinforces strategic and pilgrim routes with advanced geo-technical designs. Bioengineering Solutions deploy eco-friendly slope stabilization techniques such as coir netting, vegetation seeding, and gabion walls instead of relying solely on concrete. The project also builds energy-efficient community emergency shelters along vulnerable routes and pilgrimage corridors, upgrades hospitals and PHCs to withstand earthquakes and fires, and implements Community-Based Disaster Risk Reduction (CBDRR) programs to train village task forces.

### C. Combating Forest Fires: "Pirul Lao – Paisa Pao" Scheme

The "Pirul Lao – Paisa Pao" scheme is an environmental-economic initiative that incentivizes rural youth to collect flammable pine needles (Pirul) by paying ₹50 per kg. In 2025–26, the scheme procured 5,532 tonnes worth ₹5.42 crore. The collected needles are supplied to bio-fuel plants to generate electricity and briquettes, delivering a double benefit: removing fire fuel from the forest floor while creating rural income.

### D. Carrying Capacity Mandates

The Supreme Court has mandated carrying capacity studies for all hill stations including Mussoorie, Nainital, and Joshimath. These studies aim to cap unregulated commercial construction based on local water and soil limits, with building permits now tied to carrying capacity evaluations.


### E. Tunnel Safety Reforms (Post-Silkyara)

Following the Silkyara collapse, new reforms mandate that all tunnels exceeding 1.5 km must have a parallel escape tunnel. Advanced geological scanning is now mandatory during excavation (not just before), enabling real-time detection of soft rock "shear zones" during digging.

| **Bio-Engineering on Highways** | PWD now issues tenders for "Bio-Slope Stabilization" using Vetiver grass and geo-grids instead of just concrete walls |
| **InSAR Monitoring** | Satellite radar interferometry tracks millimeter-level ground movement on Char Dham highway |
| **Glacial Lake Monitoring** | Institutionalized high-altitude surveys via ISRO/NDEM geo-portals; drain/monitor swelling lakes |

## 25.12 OPERATIONAL DIRECTIVES AND ADMINISTRATIVE PROTOCOLS

Annual pre-monsoon review meetings headed by the Chief Secretary and Chief Minister mandate safety audits of bridges, strict tracking of pilgrim footfalls, and carrying-capacity monitoring. Rapid-response administrative orders require heavy clearance machinery (JCBs) to reach landslide blocks within 15 minutes of obstruction reporting. Regular state-wide mandatory mock drills are conducted in schools, hospitals, and flood-prone settlements. Additionally, all hotels in sensitive zones are now required to connect to Sewage Treatment Plants rather than soak pits.

## 25.13 THE WAY FORWARD: STRATEGIC POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

### Structural Reforms

| Recommendation | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Himalayan-Specific EIAs** | Standardized, stringent Environmental Impact Assessment guidelines tailored to fragile mountain ecosystems — replacing copy-paste models used for plain terrain |
| **Eco-Centric Development** | Prioritize low-impact infrastructure (well-designed bridges, eco-friendly tunnels with non-invasive Boring Machines) over aggressive mountain-cutting and surface blasting |
| **Regular Geotechnical Audits** | Ban major infrastructure projects until independent panels complete comprehensive seismic, geological, and hydrological risk assessments |
| **Decentralized CBDRR** | Training local village task forces; establishing localized communication networks not reliant on vulnerable mobile towers; reviving traditional water harvesting (Naulas/Dharas) |


### Nature-Based Solutions

| Solution | Mechanism | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Bio-Engineering for Slopes** | Replace concrete retaining walls with geo-grids, steel mesh net-anchors, and Vetiver grass | Anchors topsoil naturally; allows safe rainwater drainage |
| **Eco-Sewerage Systems** | Mandate eco-sanitation and twin-pit waste systems for hotels/homestays above 1,500m | Stops wastewater softening glacial debris slopes |
| **Eco-Forestry Restoration** | Community-led programs via Van Panchayats to remove invasive pine and replant native Oak (Banj) | Restores soil moisture; revives springs; builds fire resilience |
| **Spring Rejuvenation (SARRA)** | Treat and recharge critical water sources | Stabilizes subsurface moisture; prevents land subsidence |

Uttarakhand's disaster management has evolved through three distinct phases. The pre-2013 era was characterized by reactive relief-and-rehabilitation. The period from 2013 to 2020 saw institutional building with the establishment of SDMA, SDRF, and systematic NDRF deployment. From 2021 to the present, the state has embraced technology-first and nature-based solutions including the Bhudev App, Bio-Engineering, SARRA, and the Pirul Bio-Economy. The state is now attempting to decouple economic growth from ecological destruction through monetizing hazard removal, creating dedicated technical authorities, and enforcing hard engineering changes.
