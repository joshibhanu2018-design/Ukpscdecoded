# CHAPTER 2

# The Katyuri Dynasty & Parmar Dynasty of Garhwal

## PART A: THE KARTIKEYAPUR (KATYURI) DYNASTY (700 CE – 11th Century)

The Kartikeyapur Dynasty, commonly known as the Katyuri Dynasty, holds the distinction of being the first unified historical dynasty of Uttarakhand. Their reign is widely regarded as the Golden Age of the region's architecture and sculpture.

## 2.1 FOUNDATION & KEY RULERS

The dynasty was founded by Basant Dev around 700 CE. The rulers adopted the imperial title of *Paramabhattaraka Maharajadhiraja Parameshwar*, reflecting their claim to supreme sovereignty over the region. Their first capital was established at Joshimath in Chamoli district, which was later shifted to Vaidhnath-Kartikeyapur in the Katyur Valley near Baijnath by Subhikshraj Dev (though folk ballads attribute this move to Narsingh Dev).

Three rulers stand out as particularly consequential. Ishtagan of the Nimbar Dynasty was the first to unite the entire Uttarakhand region into a single political entity; he was a Shaivite ruler and the true unifier of the land. Lalitsur Dev was the most powerful ruler and a prolific builder — the maximum number of copper plate inscriptions belong to his reign, and he was described in these plates as a *Varaha* (boar) incarnation who uplifted the earth. Bhudev was a staunch supporter of Brahmanism who opposed Buddhism and contributed heavily to the construction of the Baijnath temple.

## 2.2 ADMINISTRATION & MILITARY STRUCTURE

### Territorial Division

The Katyuri administrative system was highly centralized and hierarchical. At the apex stood the King, beneath whom the territory was divided into Bhuktis (provinces) headed by an Uparika. Each Bhukti was subdivided into Vishs (districts) managed by a Vishpati, which in turn contained Pallikas (villages) headed by a Mahattam or Sayan.

### Key Administrative Officials

The state employed a specialized bureaucracy. The Saudabhangadhikrit served as the Chief Architect for state construction projects. Border security was managed by the Prantpal (protector of state borders) and Ghattapal (protector of mountain passes). The Narapati managed river ghats and collected crossing taxes. Financial accountability fell to the Akshapatlik (Chief Auditor) and Bhogpati (tax collector). The Bhatt and Char officials were responsible for extracting begar (forced labour), while the Pramavatar or Prabhatar served as the land measurement officer.

### Military Divisions

The Katyuri military was organized into four divisions: Infantry commanded by the Gaulmika, Cavalry under the Ashvabaladhikrit, an Elephant Corps led by the Hastibaladhikrit, and a Camel Corps under the Ushtrabaladhikrit.

## 2.3 ECONOMY & TAXATION

Agriculture, minerals, and forests constituted the primary revenue sources of the Katyuri state. Land measurement followed a standardized system in which the basic units were the Dronavapam and Nali Vapam. Larger measurements included the Kulyavap (equivalent to 8 Dron) and the Kharivap (equivalent to 20 Dron).

The dynasty maintained a system of land grants known as Vishnupriti, Goonth, or Agrahar — tax-free lands donated to Brahmins or temples. This Goonth land grant system formed the foundation of the temple economy in ancient Uttarakhand and enabled the construction and maintenance of the region's remarkable sacred architecture.

## 2.4 SOCIETY, CULTURE & RELIGION

Sanskrit served as the court language, while the common people spoke Prakrit or Pali (Lokbhasha). The most significant religious event of this period was the arrival of Adi Guru Shankaracharya, who established Jyotirmath (Badrikashram) as part of his mission to re-establish Hinduism across the subcontinent. Shankaracharya is believed to have renounced his body at Kedarnath in 820 CE.

## 2.5 ARCHITECTURE — THE ZENITH OF TEMPLE CONSTRUCTION

The Katyuri period represents the architectural Golden Age of Uttarakhand, heavily influenced by the Nagara style of North Indian temple architecture. Two distinct styles emerged during this era.

The Chhatra-Type (Pagoda) style featured a distinctive wooden umbrella (*chhatra*) roof and was predominantly wood-based. Notable examples include the Kedarnath Temple, Lakhamandal, and the temples at Bageshwar. The Shikhara-Type was characterized by square, stone-built structures without wooden umbrellas, often constructed in family groups. Outstanding examples include the Dwarahat Group (Gujar Deval) and the Katarmal Sun Temple.

Among the key architectural sites, Jageshwar contains over 100 temples showcasing both Nagara and Shikara styles. Dwarahat (Gujar Deval) stands as an incomparable example of the Shikhara style. The Katarmal Sun Temple holds the distinction of being the second largest Sun Temple in India. Baijnath features the main Shiva shrine accompanied by 17 subsidiary shrines.

## 2.6 DECLINE & FRAGMENTATION

The decline of the Katyuri Dynasty was driven by multiple factors. Weak successors allowed internal administrative decay to set in. Two devastating Nepalese invasions accelerated the collapse: King Ashokchalla attacked in 1191 CE, and King Krachaldeva subjugated Katyuri and early Chand territories in 1223 CE. The dynasty eventually fractured into multiple branches, including the Rajwars of Askot and the Mallas of Doti. The last ruler, Brahmadev (also known as Veerdev), was highly tyrannical and oppressive — according to the folk ballad *Jiyarani*, his misrule was the primary internal cause of the dynasty's collapse. The rising Chand Dynasty eventually ended Katyuri supremacy entirely.

## 2.7 COPPER PLATE EVIDENCE

Copper plate inscriptions discovered at various locations provide crucial historical evidence of the Katyuri Dynasty. Plates found at Pandukeshwar document Lalit Sur Dev's rule and land grants. Those at Bageshwar, issued by King Bhudev, name eight Katyuri kings. Additional plates from Kandara provide administrative evidence, while those found at Champawat confirm the dynasty's presence in Kumaon.

## PART B: THE PARMAR (PANWAR) DYNASTY OF GARHWAL (688 CE – 1804 CE)

## 2.8 FOUNDATION & POLITICAL UNIFICATION

The Parmar (Panwar) Dynasty was founded by Kanakpal in 688 CE, with Chandpur Garhi in Chamoli as the original capital. The dynasty endured for approximately 1,100 years until 1804 CE, producing over 60 kings across its remarkable span — one of the longest-ruling dynasties in Indian history.

## 2.9 SOURCES OF PARMAR HISTORY

### Literary Sources

Several important literary works document the history of the Parmar dynasty. The *Manodaya Kavya* was composed by Bharat Kavi in Sanskrit during King Man Shah's reign. *Garhrajvansh Kavya* was written by Mola Ram, the greatest painter of the Garhwal school. *Sabha Sar*, a seven-volume text, was authored by King Sudarshan Shah under the pen name 'Surat Kavi'. Foreign accounts of the dynasty include those by William Finch, the Jahangirnama, and the Akbarnama, which document visits to Man Shah's court.

### Epigraphic Sources

Two inscriptions are particularly significant. The Dharashila Inscription (1335 AD), found near the Mandakini valley, belongs to Anantpal II. The Raghunath Temple Copper Plate (1455 AD), belonging to Jagat Pal, constitutes the first evidence of a land grant by the Parmar dynasty.

## 2.10 KING AJAY PAL (1490–1519 CE) — THE BISMARCK OF GARHWAL

The 37th ruler of the Parmar dynasty, King Ajay Pal has been historically compared to Ashoka, Napoleon, and Bismarck for his unmatched military and administrative vision. His greatest achievement was the political unification of 52 independent Garhs (forts) into the unified kingdom of 'Garhwal', with his key victory being the defeat of chieftain Kaffu Chauhan of Uppoo Garh.

Ajaypal shifted the capital twice: first from Chandpur Garhi to Devalgarh in 1512 CE, and then from Devalgarh to Srinagar in 1517 CE, which became known as the "Delhi of Uttarakhand." His economic reforms included the introduction of the *Dhuli Patha Paimana*, a standardized measurement system where 1 Patha equalled approximately 2 kilograms.

In social policy, Ajaypal instituted the Sarola Brahmin practice, whereby food cooked by Sarola Brahmins was acceptable to all varnas — effectively eliminating caste discrimination in the army's communal meals. He patronized the installation of the family deity Goddess Rajrajeshwari at Devalgarh. In his later life, Ajaypal adopted the Gorakhnath sect and earned the title 'Adinath' in the Sawari Granth.

## 2.12 KING FATEH SHAH (1664–1710) — THE GOLDEN AGE OF GARHWAL

Fateh Shah's reign is historically compared to that of Shivaji for military prowess and Akbar for court patronage. Under his rule, Garhwal reached its cultural and political zenith.

### Military Campaigns

Fateh Shah significantly expanded Garhwal's territory, capturing Paonta, Jaunsar, and Saharanpur (where he established Fateh Nagar). His most notable military engagement was the Battle of Bhangani (1688), fought against the Sikh Guru Gobind Singh. He also conducted multiple wars against the Sirmaur and Kumaon states.

### Patron of Arts & Literature

Fateh Shah adorned his court with "Nine Gems" (Navaratnas) in the manner of Akbar, assembling scholars including Suresh Anand Barthwal, Hari Datt Nautiyal, and Dhasmana. The literary output of his era was prolific: Ratan Kavi composed the *Fateh Prakash*, which described the king as "Protector of Hindus"; Matiram authored the literary classic *Vritta Kaumudi*; and Ramchandra wrote *Fatehshah Yashovarnan* in praise of the king.

### Religious Tolerance

Fateh Shah demonstrated remarkable religious tolerance by inviting the Sikh Guru Ram Rai to Dehradun in 1676 and donating three villages — Khudbuda, Rajpur, and Chamasari — for the construction of the famous Dehradun Gurudwara.

## 2.13 PARMAR ADMINISTRATION & JUSTICE

### Central Administration

The King served as the supreme authority and was revered as an incarnation of God, earning the title *Bolanda Badrish*. Below the king, the Mukhtar or Wazir functioned as the Chief Minister. The Diwan handled policies and also served military and Senapati duties. The Daftari was in charge of Royal Orders, while the Bakshi served as the dispatcher and messenger.

### Territorial Division

The Parmar state was organized into Mandals or Parganas headed by a Faujdar or military officer. These were subdivided into Pattis managed by a Sayana or Kanungo, which in turn comprised villages administered by a Pradhan and Patwari.

### Justice System

Justice operated on three levels. At the village level, Panchayats handled local disputes. At the district level, a Chief Court functioned under the authority of the Diwan. At the supreme level, the King held absolute judicial authority. Severe punishments included confiscation of property and exile. It should be noted that a codified formal law system — the *Narendra Hindu Law* — was compiled much later, in 1918 by Harikrishna Raturi during the Tehri phase.

## 2.14 PARMAR ECONOMY & LAND SYSTEM

### Land Grant Types

The Parmar dynasty maintained several categories of land grants. Vishnupriti, Goonth, or Agrahar grants provided tax-free land donated to Brahmins or temples. Raut grants rewarded soldiers for exceptional bravery. Jagir grants provided land to state officials in lieu of salary.

### Taxation System

| Tax | Description |
|---|---|
| **Tihar** | Primary land tax — 1/3 of agricultural production |
| **Sirti** | Cash tax |
| **Paud Tuti** | Import-export tax on goods moving through borders |
| **Dand** | Monetary fine |
| **Bhent** | Gifts/tributes given to King by subordinates |

### Measurement System

The Parmar state employed the Dhuli Patha system for grain measurement, introduced by Ajaypal, where 1 Patha equalled approximately 2 kilograms. Land was measured in units of Jyula, Nali, and Mutthi.

## 2.15 GARHWAL SCHOOL OF PAINTING & CULTURE

### The Garhwal School of Painting

The Garhwal School of Painting originated from Mughal and Kangra artistic influences, initiated when the fugitive prince Suleiman Shikoh brought the painters Shamdas and Hardas to Srinagar. The school reached its peak under Fateh Shah and his successors. Its greatest exponent was Mola Ram Tomar (1743–1833), who founded the Manmath Panth and authored the *Garhraj Vansh Kavya*. His most celebrated paintings include *Chandramukhi* and *Dampati*.

### Civic Architecture — Unique Elements

Garhwal developed distinctive civic architectural elements. Tharps were square platforms constructed near temples for panchayat meetings. Mandas served as travel route mandaps or rest houses. Mangras and Baus were water channels and tanks. Chhinas were water tunnels cut through mountains, the most famous being the Maletha canal.

## 2.16 GORKHA INVASION & FALL OF THE PARMAR DYNASTY (1804)

The fall of the 1,100-year-old Parmar dynasty was not a sudden military defeat but the culmination of severe internal decay, natural disasters, and external manipulation.

Multiple factors contributed to the decline. Natural disasters struck repeatedly — a severe famine known as *Ikavani Bavani* hit in 1795, followed by a catastrophic 9.0 magnitude earthquake on 1 September 1803 that destroyed much of the kingdom's infrastructure. The treasury was emptied by continuous calamities, looting by step-brothers, and heavy annual tributes of Rs 25,000 paid to the Gorkhas following the 1791 Battle of Langurgarh. Internal conspiracies between the Khanduri and Dobhal ministerial factions and the rebellion by Parakram Shah further weakened the state. The Kumaoni Diwan Harshdev Joshi, known as the 'Chanakya of Kumaon', actively invited and assisted the Gorkhas in their invasion.

The Gorkha forces under Amar Singh Thapa and Hastidal Chautariya launched their attack in 1803. In desperation, King Pradyumna Shah sold the royal throne and jewelry and raised a makeshift army in Saharanpur. He was martyred at the Battle of Khurbura on May 14, 1804, bringing the Parmar dynasty to its tragic end.

## 2.17 ESTABLISHMENT OF TEHRI STATE (Brief Introduction)

Following the British defeat of the Gorkhas in the Anglo-Gorkha War of 1815, the territory was divided. The British retained eastern Garhwal (Pauri) under direct rule, while the western trans-Alaknanda region was handed to Sudarshan Shah, the surviving Parmar heir. The Tehri State was formally established on December 28, 1815, with its capital at the confluence of the Bhagirathi and Bhilangana rivers.
