# CHAPTER 4

# British Rule in Uttarakhand (1815–1947)

## 4.1 ARRIVAL & TERRITORIAL DIVISION (1815)

Following the defeat of the Gorkhas and the Treaty of Sugauli (1815), the British East India Company took control of the region. The territory was divided into two parts: British Kumaon and Garhwal (east of the Alaknanda River) came under direct British rule and was included in the Kumaon Commissionerate, while the western trans-Alaknanda region (excluding Dehradun and Rawain) was handed to the Parmar King Sudarshan Shah as the Tehri Riyasat (Princely State). The Alaknanda River thus served as the natural boundary between British territory and the Tehri Princely State.

The initial period of British rule (1815–1857) was characterized as a time of subdued governance, peace, and dynamism — a stark contrast to the brutal Gorkha era, which had conditioned the traumatized local population to perceive British administration as liberal and reformist.

## 4.3 COMMISSIONER G.W. TRAILL — DETAILED PROFILE

G.W. Traill was the man who built the administrative DNA of British Uttarakhand. His judicial policy was characteristically iron-fisted, encapsulated in the phrase *"Na Vakeel, Na Daleel, Na Appeal"* (No lawyer, no argument, no appeal).

In 1819, Traill created the Revenue Police system by establishing nine posts of Patwari — officials responsible for revenue collection, land records, and police powers simultaneously. In 1824, he introduced the Double Lock System for treasury security, whereby one key was held by the Collector and another by the Treasurer. To address the exploitative Coolie Begar system, he established the 'Mule Army' (Khachchar Sena) in 1822 to transport goods, reducing the burden on forced labourers.

Traill's forest policy began in 1826 when he reserved 'Thapla' land for government use and banned the felling of Sal trees — laying the first foundation for government-controlled forests in the region. He also implemented postal service across Uttarakhand starting in 1816, and oversaw construction of the Almora Jail (1816) and Pauri Jail (1821). His landmark land settlement, the Assi Sala Settlement of 1823, achieved the first precise boundary demarcation of the region.

## 4.4 COMMISSIONER SIR HENRY RAMSAY — DETAILED PROFILE

Sir Henry Ramsay earned the title 'Uncrowned King of Kumaon' during his unprecedented 28-year tenure (1856–1884), the longest of any Commissioner. His era is regarded as the Golden Age of British rule in the region.

Ramsay managed the impact of the 1857 Revolt by declaring martial law in Kumaon and utilizing the loyalty of local zamindars, keeping the revolt's impact extremely low in the region. In 1858, he abolished the destructive forest contractor system and initiated scientific management practices including tree marking, fire protection, and planned plantation. He formally expanded and codified the Revenue Police System in 1874, a system that currently covers approximately 60 per cent of Uttarakhand. In 1871, he established the Ramsay Collegiate School in Almora — Kumaon's first inter college.

## 4.5 LAND REVENUE SETTLEMENTS

### Land Ownership Structure

The land ownership structure under British rule comprised four tiers. The Thaat referred to the land itself. Thaatwans were the primary landowners. Khaikars were farmers cultivating under the Thaatwan. Sirtans were tenant farmers paying annual rent.

Over the course of British rule, eleven land settlements were conducted, of which three proved revolutionary in their impact.

### The Three Revolutionary Settlements

| Settlement | Year | Conducted By | Also Called | Key Innovation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **5th (Assi Sala)** | 1823 | G.W. Traill | Panchsala (occurred in Samvat 1880) | First time boundaries of land, forests, and water precisely demarcated within village limits |
| **8th (Bees Sala)** | 1840 (Garhwal) / 1844 (Kumaon) | J.H. Batten | Twenty-year settlement | Introduced Khasra survey method for land measurement |
| **9th (Beckett's)** | 1863–1873 | Beckett (during Ramsay's reign) | First scientific settlement | First purely scientific settlement; classified land into 5 categories |

### Beckett's Five-Category Land Classification

Beckett's settlement introduced the first scientific classification of land in Uttarakhand, organizing it into five categories from highest to lowest quality. Talaun (or Talaon) referred to permanently irrigated land of the highest quality. Upraun Avval designated dry terraced high land of the first grade. Upraun Doyam covered irrigated land dependent on rain or rivers. Kantili (or Kateel) denoted inferior terraced land. Ijrain described barren or non-terraced stony land of the lowest quality.

## 4.6 THE REVENUE POLICE SYSTEM

The Revenue Police System was created by G.W. Traill in 1819 and later expanded and codified by Henry Ramsay in 1874. Its defining characteristic was the conferral of dual powers upon Patwaris — combining revenue collection with police functions in a single official. This unique arrangement arose because the low crime rate in the hills made maintaining a separate police force uneconomical. Originally comprising nine Patwari posts, the system operates in approximately 60 per cent of Uttarakhand to this day. No other Indian state has this system at comparable scale, and it remains studied as a unique administrative innovation combining revenue and policing in one official.

## 4.7 BRITISH FOREST MANAGEMENT — FOUR PHASES

### PHASE 1: Early Exploitation & Contract System (1815–1858)

In 1818, Traill leased the Shivalik and Bhabar forests to contractors through the Kath-Bamboo Mahals system. His 1826 Thapla Land Reservation, which reserved land for government use and banned Sal tree felling, represented the first government-controlled forests. The demand for wooden railway sleepers, beginning with the railway boom of 1853, placed enormous pressure on the region's forests. Between 1853 and 1861, Frederick Wilson — known as 'Pahari Wilson' — operated as a British timber merchant who ruthlessly felled forests and floated timber down the Alaknanda and Bhagirathi rivers. The period from 1855 to 1861 marked the absolute peak of forest destruction in Uttarakhand.

### PHASE 2: Scientific Management & Restrictive Laws (1858–1911)

Henry Ramsay's 1858 abolition of the contractor system marked a turning point. He initiated scientific management practices including tree marking, fire protection, and planned plantation. In 1864, the Forest Department was formally created under the German expert Dietrich Brandis, appointed as the first Inspector General of Forests. The First Forest Act followed in 1865, passed on Brandis's advice. A Forest School was established in Dehradun in 1878, also on Brandis's recommendation, and was renamed the Imperial Forest School in 1884.

In 1893, the Benap Notification declared all Benap (unmeasured) land as protected forests. The First Forest Policy of 1894 made hunting of wild animals and fishing strictly illegal. The Forest School was upgraded to the Imperial Forest Research Institute in 1906. Between 1911 and 1917, the Stiffe and Nelson Settlement declared an additional 3,311 square miles as reserved forests, completely stripping the local population of ancestral rights.

### PHASE 3: Forest Rebellions — Public Backlash (1904–1930)

The restrictive forest policies provoked determined public resistance. The Khas Patti (Kunjdi Van) Movement of 1904–1906 saw villagers revolting against Conservator Keshavanand for reserving forests around the Chandrabadni temple in Tehri. In 1921, widespread protest fires were set in reserved forests. Durga Devi of Someshwar became the first woman arrested in the movement, imprisoned for one month for burning Thakalodi forests, while Mohan Singh Mehta was the first man arrested from Kumaon.

The most tragic episode was the Tiladi (Rawain) Massacre of May 30, 1930. The Tehri king's Diwan, Chakradhar Juyal, ordered firing on unarmed farmers gathered at Tiladi ground on the banks of the Yamuna. This event has been called the "Jallianwala Bagh of Uttarakhand," and May 30 is observed as Martyr's Day.

### PHASE 4: Resolution & Forest Panchayats (1921–1931)

On April 13, 1921, a Forest Grievances Committee was convened under Kumaon Commissioner Percy Wyndham. The committee acknowledged villagers' suffering due to rigid "scientific forestry" and recommended returning rights, lifting bans on bamboo and grazing, and allowing forest rights up to half a mile from each village.

These recommendations culminated in the Forest Panchayat Act of 1931, which established Van Panchayats and divided forests into Reserved, Protected, and Village categories. The Van Panchayat system uniquely empowers local communities in Uttarakhand to manage forests — it continues to this day and is studied globally as a model of community forest governance.

## 4.8 ECONOMY UNDER BRITISH RULE

### Revenue Sources

The British administration derived revenue from three principal sources: land revenue collected through eleven settlements; commercial activities including forest timber, tea plantations, and canal irrigation; and the Coolie Begar system of exploitative unpaid forced labour.

### Key Economic Developments

| Year | Development | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **1822** | Mule Army (Khachchar Sena) | Established by Traill to reduce Coolie Begar |
| **1843** | First Tea Plantations | Captain Huddleston established first commercial tea gardens in Pauri and Gadoliya |
| **1854** | Upper Ganges Canal | Completed during Batten's tenure; father of project: Colonel Sir P.T. Cautley |
| **1883** | Tarai Improvement Fund | For agricultural upliftment in the plains |

The Coolie Begar system — forced unpaid labour for transporting British goods — became a major cause of resentment and eventually fuelled a powerful protest movement.

## 4.10 HEALTH & MEDICAL SYSTEM UNDER BRITISH RULE

The development of health infrastructure in British Uttarakhand proceeded gradually. In 1833, the first native doctor was appointed at Almora, marking the earliest formal healthcare in the region. A Dispensary Committee was formed in Almora in 1848 under Commissioner G.T. Lushington.

The most significant medical institution was the Imperial Bacteriological Laboratory, transferred to Mukteshwar (Nainital) by Dr. Alfred Lingard in 1893. This institution — now known as the Indian Veterinary Research Institute (IVRI) and called the "Mecca of veterinary science in India" — was where Dr. Edwards discovered the rinderpest vaccine. The State Vaccine Institute was established at Patwadangar, Nainital in 1903 (now the Institute of Biotechnology). Other important facilities included a Malaria Research Center and a TB Sanatorium at Bhowali (Nainital), which became a major tuberculosis treatment centre.
